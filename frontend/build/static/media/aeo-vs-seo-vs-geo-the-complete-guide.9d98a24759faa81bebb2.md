If you've searched for a clear explanation of AEO and GEO recently, you've probably landed on three different definitions that each sound confident and contradict each other. That's not you missing something — it's the actual state of the industry. As of 2026, there is no single agreed-upon definition separating AEO from GEO, and different vendors, agencies, and researchers use the terms differently depending on what they're selling or studying.

This guide won't pretend that disagreement doesn't exist. Instead, it'll give you the one definition everyone agrees on (SEO), the most useful working definitions for the two that are still being argued over (AEO and GEO), and — more importantly — the practical checklist that matters regardless of which label you personally prefer.

## The one everyone agrees on: SEO

**Search Engine Optimization (SEO)** is the practice of getting a page to rank well in a list of results on a traditional search engine — Google, Bing, and similar. The unit of success is a ranking position: you're trying to be link #1, #2, or #3 on a results page, so a human clicks through to your actual site.

SEO's core signals are well established after two decades: relevant keywords, backlinks, page speed, mobile-friendliness, content depth, and technical crawlability. None of that has disappeared. It's just no longer the whole picture.

## Where it gets genuinely contested: AEO and GEO

Here's the honest version of what's happening. In 2023, researchers at Princeton, Georgia Tech, and the Allen Institute for AI published a paper introducing the term **Generative Engine Optimization (GEO)** — studying how specific content changes affected whether a generative AI system would cite a source when synthesizing an answer. Around the same time, marketers and SEO practitioners started using **Answer Engine Optimization (AEO)** to describe a closely related idea: structuring content so a machine — whether a featured snippet, a knowledge panel, or an AI chat reply — could lift a self-contained answer directly out of it.

Since then, the two terms have been used in genuinely inconsistent ways across the industry:

- Some treat **AEO** as the broader term (covering any machine-generated direct answer, including traditional featured snippets) and **GEO** as the narrower one (specifically about generative AI systems like ChatGPT and Perplexity).
- Others draw the line the opposite way — treating **GEO** as the broader category (any visibility inside a generative AI response) and **AEO** as a subset focused on structured, snippet-style answers.
- Some practitioners, including a few well-known agencies, argue the two terms describe the same underlying discipline and the distinction isn't worth maintaining at all.
- Google's own 2026 documentation on optimizing for generative AI search features described that work as, functionally, still SEO — just extended to a new surface.

None of these camps is simply wrong. The terms emerged from different starting points (an academic paper vs. marketing practice) and have been pulled in different directions by people with different reasons to define them narrowly or broadly. If you see a confident, absolute claim that "AEO means X and GEO means Y, full stop" — treat it as one practitioner's framing, not an industry standard.

## The working definitions this guide uses

For the rest of this piece, here's the version we find most useful in practice — not because it's the only correct one, but because it maps cleanly onto two genuinely different things you can measure:

**Answer Engine Optimization (AEO)** — structuring content so it can be extracted and presented as a direct, self-contained answer: featured snippets, "People Also Ask" boxes, voice assistant replies, and the direct-answer portions of AI chat responses. The content itself needs to work as a standalone answer with no surrounding context required.

**Generative Engine Optimization (GEO)** — earning citation and inclusion when a generative AI system (ChatGPT, Perplexity, Google's AI Overviews) synthesizes a longer answer from multiple sources. Here, the content doesn't need to *be* the whole answer — it needs to be trustworthy, specific, and citable enough that the model chooses to pull from it and credit it.

The practical difference: AEO is about being quotable in isolation. GEO is about being one of the sources a system decides to weave together and credit.

## A side-by-side comparison

| | SEO | AEO | GEO |
|---|---|---|---|
| **What "success" looks like** | Ranking position on a results page | Being the extracted answer in a snippet/direct reply | Being cited as a source inside a synthesized AI answer |
| **Primary surfaces** | Google, Bing search results | Featured snippets, voice assistants, direct AI replies | ChatGPT, Perplexity, Google AI Overviews |
| **Unit that gets evaluated** | The whole page | A specific, extractable passage | Specific facts, claims, and framing within the content |
| **What matters most** | Keywords, backlinks, technical health | Clear, self-contained, question-first structure | Verifiable specificity, structured data, topical depth |
| **How you'd measure it** | Rank tracking, organic traffic | Snippet win rate, "position zero" appearances | Citation rate and prominence in AI-generated answers |

## Do you need to choose one?

No — and this is the part that matters more than the terminology debate. The tactics that make content work for AEO and GEO overlap heavily with each other, and they build on top of good SEO rather than replacing it. A page that's poorly structured, thin on real information, or hard for a crawler to access won't perform well under any of these labels.

In practice, most of the actual work is shared:

- **Write the second paragraph like it could stand alone.** Both AEO and GEO reward content that makes a specific, verifiable claim early and clearly — not just an SEO-optimized headline with vague follow-through.
- **Add structured data.** FAQ schema, HowTo schema, and clear heading hierarchies help both traditional snippet extraction and AI synthesis understand what your content is actually claiming.
- **Be specific, not just relevant.** Generative systems favor content with concrete numbers, named comparisons, and clear attribution over generic, keyword-stuffed prose — this is true whether you call it AEO or GEO.
- **Stay crawlable to AI systems specifically.** A `robots.txt` that blocks GPTBot, ClaudeBot, or PerplexityBot removes you from consideration entirely, regardless of how well the content itself is written. Check this before anything else.
- **Keep doing real SEO.** Backlinks, technical performance, and topical authority still feed into whether any of these systems trust your site enough to retrieve from it in the first place.

## The honest takeaway

The label matters less than the mechanics. Whether your team calls this work AEO, GEO, or just "SEO for the AI era," the actual checklist — crawlable, specific, well-structured, genuinely trustworthy content — is the same. Don't let the terminology debate become a reason to delay doing the work.
